The AI Stack: From Electrons to Answers

When you ask a chatbot a question, the answer comes from ten layers of technology stacked on top of each other, from the power plant to the text on your screen. This page climbs the stack from the bottom up.

Layer 1 of 10 · Foundation

Energy

Every answer an AI gives starts as electricity, and almost all of that electricity ends up as heat.

~1.5%
of the world's electricity went to data centers in 2024 (IEA estimate). AI is the fastest-growing part.
~2×
projected growth in data-center electricity use by 2030, to roughly 950 TWh (IEA base case)
~120 kW
for one of the densest AI racks (NVIDIA NVL72), versus roughly 5–15 kW for a traditional server rack

What it is

Power plants (gas, nuclear, hydro, wind, solar and coal) feed a high-voltage grid. Substations step the voltage down, and inside the data center, backup batteries and power distribution units feed the racks. The chips turn that electricity into computation. Then the cooling system removes the heat, which is where nearly every joule ends up.

Why it matters

Power has become one of AI's main bottlenecks. As of 2026, the largest new AI campuses are planned at a gigawatt or more; one gigawatt is about the output of a large nuclear reactor. In many regions a new grid connection of that size takes years, and big transformers and gas turbines have long waiting lists. Where the power comes from also decides most of AI's operating carbon footprint.

How it connects

↓ Below Nothing but physics: fuel, falling water, wind, sunlight and fission.

↑ Above Data centers are designed around power and cooling first. Sites are increasingly chosen by where electricity is available, not where users are.

Analogy A data center is a giant electric heater that happens to compute.

Where it breaks a heater's goal is heat; a data center tries to make as little as possible, and its useful output (the computation) is real. The energy accounting is close to exact, though: almost every watt that goes in comes out as warm air or warm water.

Go deeper: air versus liquid cooling

Air cooling pushes chilled air through racks with fans. It is simple and familiar, but air carries heat poorly, so it becomes impractical somewhere around 20–40 kW per rack.

Direct-to-chip liquid cooling bolts cold plates onto the hottest chips and pumps coolant through them. Water holds roughly 3,500 times more heat than the same volume of air, which is why today's 100+ kW AI racks are almost all liquid-cooled. Immersion cooling goes further by dunking whole servers in non-conductive fluid.

Water use is a separate question. Many sites reject heat through evaporative cooling towers, which consume water. Closed-loop and dry coolers save water but use more electricity, so operators trade one resource against the other depending on climate.

Go deeper: what "PUE" means

Power usage effectiveness is total facility power divided by the power that reaches the IT equipment. A PUE of 1.0 would mean zero overhead. The largest cloud operators report about 1.1–1.2; the industry-wide average is closer to 1.5 (surveys, approximate).

PUE says nothing about how useful the computing is. A perfectly efficient building full of idle chips still has a great PUE.

Go deeper: how big is a gigawatt?

One gigawatt, running continuously, is about 8.8 terawatt-hours a year. The average US home draws about 1.2 kW averaged over the year, so a 1 GW campus uses roughly as much power as 800,000 homes (rough comparison).

Operators are signing deals for nuclear power, restarting retired plants, and building on-site gas generation. Much of the new nuclear capacity (such as small modular reactors) is still years away as of 2026.

Layer 2 of 10 · Infrastructure

Data centers

A data center turns electricity, cooling and networking into one enormous computer.

100,000+
accelerators in the largest training clusters (as of 2026; several are planned at hundreds of thousands)
~2 miles
of copper cable, about 5,000 cables, inside one 72-GPU NVIDIA NVL72 rack (vendor figure)

What it is

Rows of racks in a secured building. Each rack holds servers; each AI server holds 4 to 8 accelerator chips. Switches link the racks into a network fabric, and the building's power and cooling plant keeps the whole thing running around the clock.

Why it matters

Training a frontier model means tens of thousands of chips working on one job in lock-step, swapping results many times a second. How fast and how reliably they can talk to each other decides how much of the expensive hardware actually gets used.

How it connects

↓ Below Power and cooling set how densely chips can be packed.

↑ Above Silicon chips are the payload. The building exists to feed them electricity, remove their heat, and wire them together.

Analogy An orchestra: thousands of players who must stay in time. One slow player, such as a lagging chip or a flaky cable, holds up everyone.

Where it breaks orchestra players play different parts. In the most common training setup, thousands of chips run the same computation on different slices of data, then average what they learned.

Go deeper: two networks, two speeds

Scale-up links connect GPUs inside a server or rack. NVIDIA's NVLink gives each current-generation GPU about 1.8 terabytes per second to its neighbors (counting both directions).

Scale-out links connect racks to each other over InfiniBand or Ethernet, at about 400–800 gigabits per second per GPU in each direction, roughly 50–100 gigabytes per second. Compared direction for direction, that's about 10–20 times slower, so engineers place the chattiest parts of a computation inside a rack and the least chatty parts across racks.

Go deeper: the fabric

Big clusters use layered "fat-tree" (Clos) networks: tiers of switches arranged so any GPU can reach any other at close to full speed. Long runs use optical transceivers that turn electrical signals into light. The biggest clusters contain hundreds of thousands of them (est.), and each one is a part that can fail.

Go deeper: training sites versus inference sites

Training wants one giant, tightly connected cluster in one place, because every chip talks to every other chip constantly. Serving answers (inference) splits into independent requests, so it can be spread across many smaller data centers closer to users.

Layer 3 of 10 · Manufacturing

Silicon

Chips are made by printing tens of billions of tiny switches onto ultra-pure silicon, using machines so complex that only one company builds the most important one.

13.5 nm
wavelength of the extreme-ultraviolet (EUV) light used to print the finest features; visible light is 400–700 nm
~$200M
for one ASML EUV scanner; the newest "High-NA" version costs roughly $350M+ (reported prices)
~90%
of the world's most advanced logic chips are made by TSMC, mostly in Taiwan (common estimate)

What it is

Quartz sand is reduced to silicon, then purified to about 99.9999999% ("nine nines"). A single crystal is pulled from the melt and sliced into 300 mm wafers. Each wafer then goes through hundreds to more than a thousand steps: deposit a thin film, coat it with light-sensitive resist, print a pattern with light, etch, implant, polish, and repeat for dozens of layers. The trip takes around three months. Finally the wafer is tested and cut into individual chips (dies).

Why it matters

Smaller, better transistors mean more computation per watt and per dollar, and a large share of AI's hardware progress traces back to this layer. It is also the most concentrated part of the stack: a handful of companies in a handful of places make the parts nobody else can.

How it connects

↓ Below Fabs themselves need vast, uninterruptible power and ultra-pure water, and they're housed in some of the most controlled buildings on Earth.

↑ Above The dies cut from these wafers become GPUs, TPUs and the stacked memory chips beside them.

Analogy Lithography is like a slide projector run in reverse: a master pattern (the mask) is shrunk four times and projected onto a wafer coated with light-sensitive chemical, like exposing photographic paper.

Where it breaks EUV light is absorbed by glass and even by air, so there are no lenses. The light bounces off a chain of ultra-smooth mirrors inside a vacuum. And each chip needs dozens of patterned layers stacked with alignment errors of just a few nanometers.

Go deeper: how an EUV machine makes light

Tiny droplets of molten tin fall through a chamber about 50,000 times a second. A powerful CO₂ laser hits each one, turning it into a plasma hotter than the surface of the Sun, which glows at 13.5 nm. Mirrors from Zeiss collect and steer that light. Zeiss says that if one of these mirrors were scaled up to the size of Germany, its largest bump would be about 0.1 mm high.

A scanner is roughly the size of a bus and ships in dozens of containers. ASML in the Netherlands is the only supplier.

Go deeper: what "3 nm" actually means

Node names like "3 nm" and "2 nm" are marketing labels. No feature on a "3 nm" chip is 3 nm wide; transistor gates sit roughly 45–50 nm apart. The names keep counting down because density and efficiency keep improving, now mostly by changing the transistor's 3-D shape: from FinFETs to "gate-all-around" nanosheets, which Samsung introduced at its 3 nm node in 2022 and TSMC and Intel adopt at their 2 nm-class nodes arriving in 2025–26.

Go deeper: why the biggest chips are two chips glued together

A scanner can print an area of at most about 26 × 33 mm (roughly 850 mm²) in one exposure, so no single die can be larger. Bigger dies also catch more defects. About 60 H100-sized dies fit on a 300 mm wafer before accounting for defects.

So the newest GPUs join two large dies and several memory stacks on a shared base using advanced packaging (such as TSMC's CoWoS). Packaging capacity, not chipmaking, was one of the tightest AI bottlenecks in 2023–25.

Layer 4 of 10 · Hardware

Chips

AI runs on chips built to do enormous numbers of simple multiplications at once, and they spend much of their time waiting on memory, not math.

~1015
16-bit operations per second (a quadrillion) for an H100-class GPU from 2022–23; newer chips do several times more at lower precision
~300
math operations an H100 must do per byte it reads from memory to stay fully busy (≈ 1,000 TFLOPS ÷ 3.35 TB/s)

What it is

CPUs have a few dozen to about two hundred powerful cores, great at branching, step-by-step logic. GPUs have thousands of simpler cores plus tensor cores that each multiply small blocks of numbers in a single step. TPUs and other custom accelerators go further, laying out a grid of multiply-add units that pass numbers to their neighbors like a bucket brigade (a "systolic array").

Beside the processor sit stacks of high-bandwidth memory (HBM): 8 to 12 memory dies piled up and wired with thousands of connections, holding roughly 80–300 GB per chip.

Why it matters

The vast majority of a neural network's work is matrix multiplication, and every element of a matrix product can be computed independently. Chips that do this in massive parallel set the ceiling on how big and fast AI can be. The catch is feeding them: numbers have to come from memory, and memory has not kept pace.

How it connects

↓ Below Made from silicon wafers. Each top-end chip draws about 700–1,400 W, which drives the rack and cooling design.

↑ Above Software has to arrange every computation so these units stay fed. Idle tensor cores are wasted money.

Analogy A CPU is a few brilliant chefs who can cook anything. A GPU is thousands of line cooks, all chopping at once.

Where it breaks the line cooks aren't independent. Groups of GPU cores must execute the same instruction at the same moment, so a GPU is only fast when the work is uniform. Matrix math happens to be perfectly uniform.

Go deeper: why matrix math, specifically

A layer of a neural network takes a list of numbers (a vector) and multiplies it by a grid of learned weights (a matrix). Process many tokens at once and the vector becomes a matrix too. Multiplying a 16,000 × 16,000 matrix by one vector is about 256 million multiply-adds, and none of them depends on another, so they can all run in parallel.

Go deeper: precision, or why fewer bits helps

Numbers can be stored with 32, 16, 8 or even 4 bits. Fewer bits mean more operations per second and less memory traffic, at some cost in accuracy. As of 2026, training mostly uses 16- and 8-bit formats, and serving increasingly uses 8- or 4-bit. Much of the headline speedup between chip generations comes from dropping precision, so compare like with like.

Go deeper: fighting the memory wall

Over 20 years, a 3× versus 1.6× trend compounds to roughly 60,000× more compute but only about 100× more bandwidth. Engineers push back with bigger on-chip caches, stacked HBM, processing many requests together (batching) so each weight fetched is reused, fusing operations so data stays on-chip, and lower precision.

Go deeper: TPUs and custom chips

Google has run its own TPUs since 2015. Amazon (Trainium), Meta (MTIA) and Microsoft (Maia) build custom accelerators too, and startups try wafer-sized chips (Cerebras) or designs that keep the model in fast on-chip memory (Groq). They trade flexibility for efficiency on a narrower set of tasks. As of 2026 NVIDIA still sold the large majority of AI accelerators, though Google's TPUs and Amazon's Trainium carry major workloads, and AMD is the main alternative GPU maker.

Build it up, step by step

A chip only understands tiny instructions

At the very bottom, a GPU carries out simple instructions: load these numbers from memory, multiply them, add them, store the result. It has no idea what a "neural network" or a "word" is. Everything in this layer exists to translate what a researcher means, such as "multiply these two grids of numbers", into the exact instructions that keep tens of thousands of cores busy, and then to do that across thousands of chips at once.

Follow one line of code down

The single most common operation in AI is a matrix multiplication: multiplying a grid of inputs by a grid of learned weights. In PyTorch a researcher writes it as y = x @ W. Step through what happens underneath that one line.

What a framework gives you

Frameworks such as PyTorch and JAX supply four things that make modern AI practical:

  • Tensors. Grids of numbers of any shape, stored on the GPU. A batch of sentences is a 3-D grid: sentences × tokens × numbers per token.
  • Building blocks. Ready-made layers such as attention and feed-forward networks, so a whole transformer fits in a few hundred lines of code.
  • Automatic gradients. The framework records every operation and can replay the record backward to work out how each weight should change. Layer 8 explains why that is the heart of training.
  • Optimizers and plumbing. The code that actually nudges the weights, saves checkpoints and streams data in.

Why one GPU isn't nearly enough

Some quick arithmetic for training a 405-billion-parameter model:

Weights alone, at 2 bytes each~0.8 TB
Plus gradients, a high-precision master copy and the optimizer's running averages (~16 bytes per parameter in all)~6.5 TB
Memory on one H100 GPU80 GB
GPUs needed just to hold that, before any working space~80+
Total work: 3.8 × 1025 operations at ~4 × 1014 useful operations per second~3,000 years on one GPU
…spread over 16,000 GPUs~2–3 months

So both the model and the work have to be split across thousands of chips, and those chips have to stay perfectly coordinated. That's the job of distributed-training software.

Split the work, then stay in sync

The figure below shows the four main ways to split a model. Real runs combine them. Whatever the split, each training step ends with a synchronization: groups that trained on different data exchange their gradients and average them in an operation called an all-reduce, and then every copy applies the identical update so that all copies stay the same. Libraries such as NVIDIA's NCCL run this exchange over the fast links inside a rack and the network fabric between racks (Layer 2).

Recap: what it is, why it matters, how it connects

What it is

From the bottom: a driver lets the operating system talk to the chip. A programming platform such as NVIDIA's CUDA (since 2007) or AMD's ROCm lets programmers write kernels, small programs that run on thousands of cores at once. Libraries supply tuned kernels for matrix math and for chip-to-chip communication. Compilers (XLA, Triton, torch.compile) fuse and generate kernels automatically. Frameworks such as PyTorch and JAX let researchers write models as math and compute gradients for them automatically.

Why it matters

The same hardware can run two or three times faster or slower depending on the software. CUDA's head start, with nearly two decades of tools, libraries and trained programmers, is a big reason NVIDIA leads. And distributed-training software is what makes 16,000 separate chips behave like one computer.

How it connects

↓ Below Exposes the chips' tensor cores, memory and interconnect.

↑ Above Lets researchers describe the model and its training in a few hundred lines of code instead of hardware instructions.

Analogy A logistics company: the framework is the order desk, the compiler plans routes, kernels are the trucks, and the communication library is the highway between warehouses.

Where it breaks a late truck delays one package. In synchronized training, one slow GPU delays every other GPU at every step, so the whole job moves at the pace of its slowest part.

Go deeper: what a kernel is, and why fusing them helps

A kernel is one GPU program, such as "multiply these two matrices." Every time a kernel finishes, its results go back to memory, and the next kernel reads them again. Fusing several steps into one kernel keeps data on-chip. FlashAttention (2022) is the famous example: the same math as standard attention, reorganized to avoid memory round-trips, and several times faster.

Go deeper: automatic differentiation

Frameworks record every operation in the model's forward pass. To train, they replay that record backward to compute how the error depends on every weight. That's backpropagation (Layer 8), reduced to a single line of code such as loss.backward().

Go deeper: alternatives to CUDA

AMD's ROCm, Google's XLA (which drives TPUs through JAX and PyTorch), OpenAI's Triton kernel language (which targets multiple vendors), Apple's MLX and Intel's oneAPI. Switching frameworks is fairly easy; matching years of hand-tuned kernels is the hard part.

Go deeper: keeping a giant job alive

With tens of thousands of parts, something is always failing: a GPU, a memory chip, a cable, a power supply. Training jobs save checkpoints regularly and restart automatically. A nastier problem is "silent data corruption," where a faulty chip returns wrong numbers without crashing.

Data

Build it up, step by step

Text becomes practice questions, for free

A pretraining example isn't a question paired with an answer. It's just a chunk of text, typically a few thousand tokens long (Llama 3 used chunks of about 8,000), cut from the dataset. Every position in the chunk is a small test: given everything before this point, what comes next? So an 8,000-token chunk holds 8,000 practice questions, and a 15-trillion-token dataset holds 15 trillion. Nobody has to label anything, which is why training can use so much raw text. Layer 8 shows one of these tests being graded.

Where the text comes from

  • The web. Crawls by Common Crawl and by labs' own crawlers, which are supposed to honor sites' opt-out instructions.
  • Code. Public software repositories, which also seem to help with step-by-step reasoning.
  • Books, papers and reference works, plus data licensed from publishers and other companies.
  • Synthetic data. Text generated by other models, such as worked math solutions checked for correctness, or good documents rewritten in different styles. It fills gaps where human text is scarce, but models trained heavily on unfiltered model output can degrade, so it's filtered and mixed carefully.

The mix is a deliberate recipe

Labs choose proportions carefully because the mix shapes the model's skills. Meta published an unusually specific breakdown for Llama 3:

  • General knowledge~50%
  • Math and reasoning~25%
  • Code~17%
  • Other languages~8%

Near the end of training, labs often switch to a smaller set of especially high-quality text for a final stretch (called annealing), which measurably improves results.

A second, much smaller kind of data

After pretraining, models learn from carefully made data (Layer 8 shows how it's used):

  • Demonstrations: prompts paired with high-quality answers, written by people or generated by models and checked. Thousands to millions of examples.
  • Comparisons: two answers to the same prompt and a judgment of which is better, made by people or by an AI following written principles.
  • Checkable problems: math with known answers, code with tests, puzzles with verifiable solutions.

These sets are tiny next to the pretraining data, yet they decide much of how the finished assistant behaves.

Recap: what it is, why it matters, how it connects

What it is

Sources include web crawls (the nonprofit Common Crawl has collected hundreds of billions of pages since 2008), code, books, scientific papers, encyclopedias, licensed data, transcripts, and synthetic text generated by other models. A pipeline extracts the text, identifies the language, removes duplicates, filters for quality, strips unsafe content and personal information, and mixes sources in chosen proportions. Finally a tokenizer turns the text into a sequence of integers.

Why it matters

A model can only reflect what's in its data. Cleaning often matters as much as quantity, and the mix shapes skills: more code makes better coders. High-quality public human text is also finite; researchers at Epoch AI have estimated it could be fully used by frontier training sometime between roughly 2026 and 2032.

How it connects

↓ Below Collected, stored and processed on the same infrastructure. Tokenizer choices get baked into the model permanently.

↑ Above Tokens become the model's inputs (Layer 7), and each next token is the answer key during training (Layer 8).

Analogy Tokens are like syllables for a machine: common words are one piece, rare words get broken into chunks.

Where it breaks token boundaries follow statistics of the training text, not sound or meaning. "dog", " dog" (with a space) and "Dog" can be three different tokens.

Go deeper: how filtering works

Exact and near-duplicate pages are removed (the web repeats itself enormously). Simple rules drop pages that are too short, mostly symbols, or boilerplate. Classifiers trained to recognize "educational" or well-written text score the rest. In typical published pipelines, most of the raw crawl is thrown away, often around 90% (varies widely).

Go deeper: how a tokenizer learns its vocabulary

Byte-pair encoding (BPE) starts with the 256 possible byte values, then repeatedly merges the most frequent neighboring pair into a new token, until it reaches a target vocabulary size, commonly 32,000 to 256,000 (Llama 3 uses about 128,000). Frequent words end up as single tokens; rare words stay in pieces.

Go deeper: why tokens cause odd mistakes

Ask a model how many r's are in "strawberry" and it may struggle, because it sees a couple of tokens, not ten letters. Long numbers are split into awkward chunks, which complicates arithmetic. Many non-English languages need more tokens for the same meaning, so they cost more and fit less into the model's memory.

Layer 7 of 10 · Architecture

The model

A model is a huge stack of simple arithmetic, mostly weighted sums, and its billions of weights were learned rather than written by anyone.

405 billion
parameters in Llama 3.1 405B, about 810 GB at 16 bits each. Most frontier labs don't disclose sizes.
37 of 671B

Build it up, step by step

The big picture: build once, use many times

Two phases are easy to blur together, so separate them first.

Building the model happens once and takes months. A training program reads trillions of words and slowly adjusts a huge list of numbers. When it finishes, the result is literally a file of numbers, called the weights or parameters: hundreds of gigabytes for a large model.

Using the model happens every time you send a message and takes seconds. Your text is turned into numbers and pushed through a fixed recipe of arithmetic that uses those weights. Out comes a probability for every possible next piece of text. One piece is picked and added to the text, and the whole thing runs again.

Building the model once · months · enormous compute

  1. Trillions of words of text
  2. Training adjusts the numbers, step by step
  3. A file of numbers: the weights

the same weights are loaded onto GPUs ↓

Using the model every message · seconds

  1. Your text, as tokens
  2. Arithmetic using the weights
  3. A probability for every next token
  4. Pick one, add it, repeat

So a model is not a database of stored sentences, and it is not a program of rules that someone wrote. It is arithmetic whose billions of numbers were tuned by example. The rest of this layer builds that arithmetic up from scratch.

A model is a function with knobs

Start tiny. Say you want to predict an apartment's monthly rent from its size. A simple guess: rent = w × size + b. The numbers w (dollars per square foot) and b (a base amount) are parameters: knobs you can turn. Each setting draws a different line.

Learning means finding the knob settings that make predictions match known examples. We score the mismatch with a single number, the loss; here it's the typical size of the error (the red lines). Drag the knobs yourself, then press Auto-fit. The computer repeatedly nudges each knob in whichever direction lowers the loss. That's gradient descent, and it is exactly how large models are trained, just with billions of knobs instead of two.

A neuron: a weighted sum, then a bend

An artificial neuron is the same idea with more inputs. It multiplies each input by its own weight, adds them up along with a bias, then applies a simple bend. The most common bend, called ReLU, just turns negative results into zero.

Why bend at all? Without it, stacking neurons only ever gives another straight line, because a weighted sum of weighted sums is still a weighted sum. The bend is what lets a network represent curves, thresholds and "only if" behavior. Try the weights below: thicker lines are bigger weights, and dashed lines are negative ones.

A network: many bends add up to any shape

Put many neurons side by side and you have a layer. Feed one layer's outputs into another and you have a network. In the small network below, each hidden neuron contributes one bend (the blue dots), and the output adds those bends together, so it's made of straight pieces joined at the bends. With enough of them, a network can approximate almost any shape.

This is a real network being trained live in your browser, from random starting weights, by the same nudging as in Step 2. Watch the loss fall. Then try it with 2 neurons versus 20.

A large language model is this recipe at enormous scale. Instead of one input number it takes thousands of numbers per token. Instead of one hidden layer it has around a hundred stacked blocks. Instead of one output it produces a score for every token in its vocabulary. The principle doesn't change: weighted sums, bends, and weights tuned by gradient descent.

Turning words into numbers: embeddings

Networks only do arithmetic, so text has to become numbers. The tokenizer (Layer 6) turns text into token IDs, but an ID like 1282 is just a label; its size means nothing. So the model keeps a big table with one row per token in its vocabulary. Each row is a list of numbers called an embedding: 4,096 numbers per token in Llama 3 8B, and 16,384 in the 405B model. The model's first step is to look up each token's row.

The rows start out random and are tuned during training like every other weight. Tokens used in similar ways get nudged in similar directions, so related words end up with similar lists of numbers: close together, if you picture each list as a point in space.

Predicting the next word, the naive way

A language model has exactly one job: given the text so far, give a probability for every possible next token. The simplest possible version just counts. Read some text, record which words followed which, and turn the counts into probabilities. Pick a word to see what tends to follow it, or generate a sentence by repeatedly sampling a next word.

The output is locally sensible but goes nowhere, because each choice looks back only one word. To finish "The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too…" you need to connect "it" to words far back. Real models solve that with attention. They also don't count words; they compute the probabilities with a network, which lets them handle sentences they've never seen.

Attention: letting each word gather context

Attention lets every token pull in information from the earlier tokens that matter to it. Using learned weights, each token computes three short lists of numbers from its embedding:

  • a query: what am I looking for?
  • a key: what do I contain?
  • a value: what would I pass along?

Then it takes three moves: score each earlier token by how well its key matches the query; weigh the scores into percentages that add up to 100%; and mix the earlier tokens' values in those proportions. Here it is with made-up two-number vectors, from the point of view of "it" in "The cat sat because it".

In a real model the vectors have about 128 numbers each. Every layer runs dozens of these "attention heads" side by side, each with its own learned weights and its own idea of what to look for, and every token does this at the same time. The interactive figure below shows the pattern one idealized head might produce across a whole sentence.

A transformer block, and the running notes

A transformer stacks the same block many times. Picture each token carrying its own running set of notes: a vector that starts as its embedding and flows upward through the model. Researchers call it the residual stream. Each block does two things, and each one adds what it found to the notes instead of replacing them:

  • Attention (Step 7): each token gathers relevant information from earlier tokens. This is the only place tokens exchange information.
  • Feed-forward network (Step 4): each token, on its own, runs its notes through a wide layer of neurons. This part holds most of the parameters, and much of what the model "knows" seems to be stored here.

Early blocks tend to deal with local details such as word pieces and grammar; later blocks build more abstract features. The same two operations, repeated from a few dozen to over a hundred times, are essentially all there is.

From notes to a prediction

After the last block, the final notes of the last token are compared against a second big table with one row per vocabulary token (some models reuse the embedding table for this). That comparison gives one score, called a logit, for each of more than 100,000 tokens. A softmax turns the scores into probabilities that add up to 100%, and a sampler picks one (see Temperature in Layer 9).

Every position gets a prediction like this. During training, all of them are graded at once. When generating text, only the last one is used.

What's inside, and what isn't

No sentences are stored to be looked up, and nobody wrote rules like "if asked about France, say Paris". What the model knows is spread across billions of weights, in a form researchers can only partly read. It can still memorize passages it saw many times. And because it was trained to produce likely text, not verified truth, it can state false things fluently. Layers 8 and 9 show where that comes from.

Recap: what it is, why it matters, how it connects

What it is

An artificial neuron multiplies each input by a weight, adds the results, and passes the total through a simple bend (a nonlinearity). An embedding turns each token ID into a list of thousands of numbers, a position in a kind of "meaning space." The transformer (2017) then applies the same kind of block again and again, from a few dozen to over a hundred times: an attention step, where each token gathers information from earlier tokens, and a feed-forward step, where each token is processed by wide layers of neurons. At the end, the final vector is turned into a score for every token in the vocabulary.

Why it matters

The architecture decides what the model can learn efficiently. Attention lets any word draw directly on any earlier word, however far back, and it's built from matrix math that GPUs run well. That combination is why transformers took over.

How it connects

↓ Below Every block is matrix multiplication, exactly what the chips and software are built for.

↑ Above Training sets the weights (Layer 8). Inference runs them (Layer 9).

Analogy "Neuron" is borrowed from biology, and attention is like a reader glancing back to work out what "it" refers to.

Where it breaks a brain cell is a living cell with complex chemistry and timing; an artificial neuron is one line of arithmetic. And attention runs for every word at once, in dozens of parallel "heads" per layer, most of which do things far less tidy than resolving a pronoun.

Go deeper: attention in one paragraph

Each token produces three vectors: a query ("what am I looking for?"), a key ("what do I contain?") and a value ("what will I pass along?"). A token scores every earlier token by multiplying its query with their keys, turns the scores into weights that add up to 1 (a "softmax"), and takes the weighted average of their values. Standard attention compares every token with every other, so its cost grows with the square of the text length, one reason long contexts are expensive.

Go deeper: embeddings

Llama 3.1 405B represents each token as 16,384 numbers. Directions in this space loosely capture relationships. The famous "king − man + woman ≈ queen" comes from older word-embedding models and works only approximately, on well-chosen examples. Inside a transformer, each token's vector is rewritten layer by layer as it absorbs context.

Go deeper: where the parameters live

In a typical dense transformer, two-thirds or more of the parameters sit in the feed-forward blocks and most of the rest in attention (modern attention designs share keys and values, which shrinks attention's share further). In mixture-of-experts models, the feed-forward part is split into many "experts" and a small router sends each token to just a few, so total size and per-token cost come apart.

Go deeper: can we read the weights?

Individual weights are almost never meaningful on their own. Interpretability research finds "features" (concepts such as a specific city, or code with a bug) spread across many neurons, and is beginning to trace how some behaviors are computed. Most of what a large model does is still not understood in detail.

Training

Training shows the model trillions of examples, measures how wrong each next-word guess was, and nudges every weight slightly to be less wrong.

~31 million

Build it up, step by step

Every word is a practice question

Training turns ordinary text into fill-in-the-next-word questions. Take "The cat sat on the mat." In a single pass, the model guesses at every position: after "The", what's next? After "The cat"? And so on. Each guess is a probability for every token in the vocabulary, and it's graded against the word that actually came next. Switch between an untrained and a trained model to see the difference.

Measuring wrongness: the loss

The grade for one guess is the loss: −log(probability the model gave the right answer). If the model was nearly certain and right, the loss is close to 0. If it gave the right word 1%, the loss is 4.6. A brand-new model spreads its bets roughly evenly over about 128,000 tokens, so its loss starts near 11.8.

Training has exactly one goal: make the average loss over all those practice questions as low as possible. Nothing in that goal mentions facts, grammar or helpfulness. Those emerge because they help predict text.

Passing the blame backward

Once a guess is graded, the model needs to know how to change. Backpropagation answers a question for every single weight: if I nudged this weight up a little, would the loss go up or down, and by how much? It starts at the output ("the right token's score should have been higher") and works backward layer by layer, using the chain rule from calculus to pass the blame along each connection. Weights that pushed hardest toward the wrong answer get the most blame.

The result is the gradient: one number per weight, together pointing in the direction that increases the loss fastest. Step the opposite way and the loss goes down.

Nudge every weight, a little

Gradient descent moves every weight a small step downhill, just like Auto-fit in Layer 7. The step size is the learning rate: too small and training crawls, too big and it overshoots. Try it in the toy below. In practice each step averages the gradients from a batch of millions of tokens, which makes the direction more reliable. An optimizer called Adam also adapts the step size for each weight, and the learning rate starts small, rises, then slowly decays over the run.

The whole loop, at scale

Put the pieces together and pretraining is one loop, repeated:

repeat about a million times:
    take the next batch of text        // millions of tokens
    predict every next token           // forward pass
    grade every prediction             // loss
    work out each weight's blame       // backpropagation
    nudge every weight a little        // optimizer step

For Llama 3 405B, this loop ran for a couple of months on about 16,000 GPUs. The earliest steps produce gibberish. After enough of them, the model writes fluent text. Along the way the software saves checkpoints so a crash doesn't lose weeks of work, and engineers watch the loss curve for trouble.

Why predicting words teaches so much

To lower its loss, the model has to get better at blanks like these, each of which needs a different skill:

“The capital of Japan is ___”a fact
“If you drop a glass on a stone floor, it will probably ___”everyday physics
“def area(r): return 3.14159 * r * ___”code
“17 + 25 = ___”arithmetic
“She laughed so hard at his joke that she ___”reading people

So the weights come to represent facts, grammar, styles and reusable patterns of reasoning. But the model learns whatever helps prediction, which includes the errors, biases and contradictions in its data.

From text predictor to assistant

A freshly pretrained model, called a base model, continues documents; it doesn't answer you. Here's the kind of difference post-training makes (illustrative):

PromptWhat is the capital of Australia?
Base model continues the "document"

What is the capital of Canada? What is the capital of Brazil? Test your knowledge with these 50 geography questions…

After post-training

The capital of Australia is Canberra. Many people guess Sydney, but Canberra was purpose-built as a compromise between Sydney and Melbourne.

Getting from one to the other takes three stages:

  1. PretrainingPredict the next token on trillions of tokens of text. Takes most of the compute. Result: a base model with broad knowledge.
  2. Supervised fine-tuningKeep training on example conversations: prompts paired with high-quality answers. Thousands to millions of examples. Result: it follows the assistant format.
  3. Reinforcement learningThe model answers, answers are scored, and it's nudged toward higher-scoring behavior. Much of the recent progress in step-by-step reasoning came from this stage.

Where the scores come from

Reinforcement learning needs a way to score answers. There are three main sources, and labs combine them:

  1. The model writes two answers to the same prompt.
  2. A person picks the better one, or an AI does, following a written set of principles (a "constitution").
  3. A reward model learns to predict those picks.
  4. The model is trained to produce answers the reward model scores highly.

With human picks, this is RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback). With AI picks guided by principles, it's the constitutional approach, which scales further and makes the intended values explicit. For math and code, answers can often be checked automatically against known results or tests, with no judge needed. Each source has weaknesses: models can learn to please the judge rather than be right, which is why labs test for flattery and loophole-seeking.

Recap: what it is, why it matters, how it connects

What it is

Pretraining: take a chunk of text; at every position, the model predicts a probability for every possible next token. The loss measures how little probability it gave to the token that actually came next. Backpropagation works out how each weight contributed to that error, and gradient descent nudges every weight a tiny step in the direction that reduces it. Repeat for millions of steps, each on a batch of millions of tokens.

Post-training turns a text predictor into an assistant: fine-tuning on example conversations; learning from human preferences (RLHF: people compare answers, a reward model learns their taste, and the model is optimized against it); constitutional approaches, where the model critiques and revises its own answers against a written set of principles and AI feedback replaces some human labels; and reinforcement learning on tasks with checkable answers, such as math and code.

Why it matters

This is where capabilities come from. Scaling laws, smooth and predictable improvements in loss as compute, data and size grow, are what gave labs the confidence to spend hundreds of millions of dollars on single runs.

Go deeper: loss, in numbers

The loss for one prediction is −log(probability given to the right token). Give it 50% and the loss is 0.69; give it 90% and the loss is 0.11; give it 1% and the loss is 4.6. Training pushes the average down across trillions of predictions.

Go deeper: backpropagation and the "6ND" rule

Backpropagation applies the chain rule of calculus backward from the loss through every layer. The backward pass costs about twice the forward pass, so training takes about 6 operations per parameter per token: 2 forward and 4 backward. For Llama 3.1 405B: 6 × 405 billion × ~15.6 trillion tokens ≈ 3.8 × 1025.

Go deeper: scaling laws, and their limits

Loss falls as a smooth power law as compute grows (Kaplan et al., 2020). DeepMind's "Chinchilla" work (2022) found the compute-optimal recipe was about 20 training tokens per parameter. Many recent models are trained far past that (Llama 3 8B saw about 1,900 tokens per parameter) because smaller models are cheaper to run afterward.

Smooth loss does not mean smooth abilities: specific skills can seem to appear suddenly on benchmarks, though some of that is an artifact of how the benchmarks are scored. Whether scaling keeps paying off at the same rate is an open question.

Go deeper: what training costs

31 million GPU-hours at a rental price of roughly $2 an hour is about $60 million in compute (est.). The largest frontier runs are estimated to cost hundreds of millions of dollars. Those figures exclude research, failed experiments, data and staff, which can cost as much again or more.

Go deeper: the limits of post-training

Optimizing against a reward model can teach a model to please rather than to be right (sycophancy), or to find loopholes in the reward ("reward hacking"). Constitutional and AI-feedback methods scale better than pure human labeling but inherit the blind spots of the principles and the models doing the judging.

Build it up, step by step

The whole conversation is one document

A chat looks like messages going back and forth. To the model, it's a single document with special marker tokens showing who said what. Its only task is to continue that document. When it writes the end-of-turn marker, the app stops and shows you the text.

No memory, and no learning, in between

Between your messages, nothing is running on your behalf. When you send the next message, the app sends the entire conversation again and the model reads it from the start. Providers cache the part they've already processed, which saves time and money but doesn't change the result. The weights never change as you chat. When a conversation outgrows the context window, older parts have to be dropped or summarized. "Memory" features are the app saving notes and pasting them back into the document.

The generation loop

Writing a reply is the prediction from Layer 7, run in a loop:

text = the whole conversation so far
repeat:
    run the model on text        // a probability for each of ~100,000+ tokens
    pick one token               // temperature decides how adventurous
    if it's the end-of-turn marker: stop
    add it to text, and show it to you

Thanks to the KV cache, each trip around the loop only has to push the newest token through the network. The two figures below show the loop, then the "pick one token" step up close.

Why answers can be confidently wrong

Each token is chosen because it is likely given the text so far, not because anything checked it. If a false answer is common in the training data, or the true answer is rare or obscure, the most plausible-sounding continuation can be wrong, and it comes out with the same fluency as a right one. This is called hallucination. Once a wrong token is written, later tokens build on it, because the model continues its own text.

Search and retrieval tools, careful post-training for honesty (including saying "I don't know"), and checking answers against sources all reduce this, but none eliminates it.

"Thinking" before answering

Reasoning models run the same loop, but they first write out intermediate steps, often hidden from you, before the final answer. Each token of reasoning is another full pass through the network, so writing more steps means spending more computation on the question. That lets the model break problems down, try approaches and catch some of its own mistakes. It's trained mostly with the reinforcement learning from Layer 8, on problems whose answers can be checked. The cost is time and energy: a hard question can take thousands of reasoning tokens.

Recap: what it is, why it matters, how it connects

What it is

Prefill: all the prompt's tokens go through the network in parallel, and each layer's keys and values are saved in the KV cache. Decode: to produce each new token, only the newest token runs through the network, looking back at the cache instead of recomputing it. The model outputs a probability for every token in its vocabulary; a sampler picks one (the temperature setting controls how adventurous it is); the token is appended; repeat until done.

Context windows (how much text fits at once) ranged from about 128,000 to over a million tokens for leading models as of 2026.

Why it matters

Training happens once; inference happens billions of times a day. For a popular model, the total compute spent answering questions can exceed what was spent training it. Its speed and cost decide which products are possible.

How it connects

↓ Below Each new token requires reading the model's active weights from memory, so memory bandwidth (Layer 4), more than raw math, sets the speed.

↑ Above Every application is built from inference calls.

Analogy Writing an essay one word at a time while keeping notes on everything so far. The KV cache is the notes: you never have to reread the whole thing.

Where it breaks the "notes" aren't words; they are intermediate numbers for every layer. They grow with every token, and eventually their size limits how long a context can be and how many users one chip can serve.

Go deeper: why output tokens cost more than input tokens

Prefill handles thousands of tokens in parallel, so the chip's math units stay busy. Decode handles one token at a time per request, and each step must stream the active weights from memory. Serving many users together (batching) spreads that cost, but output is still the expensive part, so providers typically price output tokens several times higher than input.

Go deeper: the math of temperature and top-p

The model outputs a score (logit) z for each token. Probabilities are proportional to ez/T, where T is the temperature. At T = 1 you get the model's own distribution; as T approaches 0 it always picks the top token ("greedy"); above 1 the distribution flattens. Top-p sampling adds a cutoff: sample only from the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities add up to p, for example 0.9.

Go deeper: KV cache arithmetic

Bytes per token = 2 (keys and values) × layers × key-value heads × head size × bytes per number. For Llama 3 70B: 2 × 80 × 8 × 128 × 2 ≈ 330 KB. Multiply by 128,000 tokens and it's about 42 GB, for one conversation. Techniques such as grouped-query attention (already counted here), cache compression and paging keep this manageable.

Go deeper: speed, latency and "thinking"

Time to first token is typically a few hundred milliseconds to a few seconds; after that, tens to a few hundred tokens per second per user. "Reasoning" models write a long hidden chain of thought before answering. That helps on hard problems but can multiply the tokens, and so the energy, by 10 to 100 times.

Go deeper: tricks that make serving cheaper

Batching many users per chip; quantization (4- or 8-bit weights); caching the shared start of prompts; speculative decoding, where a small model drafts several tokens and the big model checks them in one pass; and distillation into smaller models. As of 2026, API prices ranged from well under $1 to tens of dollars per million output tokens, so a short answer costs a fraction of a cent to a few cents.

Build it up, step by step

Everything must fit in the context window

Whatever an application wants the model to consider has to be written into the document it continues (Layer 9): instructions, the conversation, your files, search results, tool output. All of it competes for the same limited context window. Designing an AI application is largely deciding what goes in.

Tool use: the model asks, the app does

A model can't browse, run code or check the weather by itself. It's trained to write a structured request instead. The application notices the request, carries it out, and pastes the result back into the document as more text. The model then continues with that result in view.

userWhat's the weather in Paris tomorrow?
assistant{"tool": "get_forecast", "city": "Paris", "day": "tomorrow"}   ← written by the model
tool result{"high_c": 19, "low_c": 11, "rain_chance": 0.7}   ← run by the app, pasted in
assistantTomorrow in Paris: a high of 19 °C, a low of 11 °C and a 70% chance of rain, so take an umbrella.

The application tells the model which tools exist, and what inputs they take, by describing them in the system prompt. Open standards such as the Model Context Protocol (2024) give applications one common way to plug tools in.

Retrieval: bringing in your own documents

A model knows only its training data, which ends at a cutoff date and doesn't include your files. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) fixes that by searching first and answering second:

  1. in advanceSplit your documents into passages and turn each into an embedding (Layer 7, Step 5)
  2. Turn your question into an embedding too
  3. Find the passages whose embeddings are closest in meaning
  4. Paste them into the prompt with an instruction to answer from them
  5. The model answers, citing those passages

It reduces made-up answers but doesn't eliminate them: the search can miss the right passage, and the model can misread one.

Agents: tool use in a loop

An agent is a model given tools and a goal, running the tool-use cycle over and over until it decides the task is done: plan, act, look at the result, adjust. The figure below steps through a coding agent fixing a bug. Each lap is another full inference call on an ever-longer document, which is why agents are slow and token-hungry, and why a small error rate per step compounds.

Keeping it reliable and safe

Because outputs are probabilistic and can be wrong, good applications wrap the model in checks:

  • Verification: run the tests, check the citation, validate the format.
  • Permissions: ask before sending an email, spending money or deleting files.
  • Guarding against injected instructions: text inside a web page or document ("ignore your instructions and…") is data, and apps and models must be built to treat it that way.
  • Evaluation: measure quality on large sets of realistic tasks before and after every change.
  • A human in the loop wherever mistakes are costly.

Open questions and limits

What this stack still can't settle

Energy demand

Projections for AI's electricity use span a wide range because they depend on chip sales, efficiency gains and demand nobody can forecast well. Efficiency per query keeps improving, but cheaper AI tends to be used more. Local grids feel the strain first, and the carbon impact depends on whether new supply is gas, nuclear or renewables.

Supply-chain concentration

One company makes EUV scanners, one foundry makes most leading-edge chips, and three companies make nearly all high-bandwidth memory. A disruption to any of them, whether a disaster, a conflict or an export rule, would ripple through the whole stack.

What models still get wrong

They can state false things fluently ("hallucination"), stumble on tasks that look trivial (counting letters, some arithmetic) because of tokens, know nothing after their training cutoff unless given tools, tell people what they want to hear, and follow instructions hidden in documents they read ("prompt injection"). Errors compound over long agent tasks.

What we don't understand

We can train models far more easily than we can explain them. Whether scaling keeps delivering, how to measure real ability rather than benchmark memorization, what happens when high-quality human text runs short, and how copyright and consent apply to training data are all unresolved.
