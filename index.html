The AI Stack: From Electrons to Answers

When you ask a chatbot a question, the answer comes from ten layers of technology stacked on top of each other, from the power plant to the text on your screen. This page climbs the stack from the bottom up.

Layer 1 of 10 · Foundation

Energy

Every answer an AI gives starts as electricity, and almost all of that electricity ends up as heat.

~1.5%
of the world's electricity went to data centers in 2024 (IEA estimate). AI is the fastest-growing part.
~2×
projected growth in data-center electricity use by 2030, to roughly 950 TWh (IEA base case)
~120 kW
for one modern AI server rack, versus roughly 5–15 kW for a traditional rack

What it is

Power plants (gas, nuclear, hydro, wind, solar and coal) feed a high-voltage grid. Substations step the voltage down, and inside the data center, backup batteries and power distribution units feed the racks. The chips turn that electricity into computation. Then the cooling system removes the heat, which is where nearly every joule ends up.

Why it matters

Power has become one of AI's main bottlenecks. As of 2026, the largest new AI campuses are planned at around a gigawatt, the output of a large nuclear reactor. In many regions a new grid connection of that size takes years, and big transformers and gas turbines have long waiting lists. Where the power comes from also decides most of AI's carbon footprint.

How it connects

↓ Below Nothing but physics: fuel, falling water, wind, sunlight and fission.

↑ Above Data centers are designed around power and cooling first. Sites are increasingly chosen by where electricity is available, not where users are.

Analogy. A data center is a giant electric heater that happens to compute.

Where it breaks: a heater's goal is heat; a data center tries to make as little as possible, and its useful output (the computation) is real. The energy accounting is close to exact, though: almost every watt that goes in comes out as warm air or warm water.

Go deeper: air versus liquid cooling

Air cooling pushes chilled air through racks with fans. It is simple and familiar, but air carries heat poorly, so it becomes impractical somewhere around 30–50 kW per rack.

Direct-to-chip liquid cooling bolts cold plates onto the hottest chips and pumps coolant through them. Water holds roughly 3,500 times more heat than the same volume of air, which is why today's 100+ kW AI racks are almost all liquid-cooled. Immersion cooling goes further by dunking whole servers in non-conductive fluid.

Water use is a separate question. Many sites reject heat through evaporative cooling towers, which consume water. Closed-loop and dry coolers save water but use more electricity, so operators trade one resource against the other depending on climate.

Go deeper: what "PUE" means

Power usage effectiveness is total facility power divided by the power that reaches the IT equipment. A PUE of 1.0 would mean zero overhead. The largest cloud operators report about 1.1–1.2; the industry-wide average is closer to 1.5 (surveys, approximate).

PUE says nothing about how useful the computing is. A perfectly efficient building full of idle chips still has a great PUE.

Go deeper: how big is a gigawatt?

One gigawatt, running continuously, is about 8.8 terawatt-hours a year. The average US home draws about 1.2 kW averaged over the year, so a 1 GW campus uses roughly as much power as 800,000 homes (rough comparison).

Operators are signing deals for nuclear power, restarting retired plants, and building on-site gas generation. Much of the new nuclear capacity (such as small modular reactors) is still years away as of 2026.

Layer 2 of 10 · Infrastructure

Data centers

A data center turns electricity, cooling and networking into one enormous computer.

100,000+
accelerators in the largest training clusters (as of 2026; several are planned at hundreds of thousands)
~2 miles
of copper cable, about 5,000 cables, inside one 72-GPU NVIDIA NVL72 rack (vendor figure)

What it is

Rows of racks in a secured building. Each rack holds servers; each AI server holds 4 to 8 accelerator chips. Switches link the racks into a network fabric, and the building's power and cooling plant keeps the whole thing running around the clock.

Why it matters

Training a frontier model means tens of thousands of chips working on one job in lock-step, swapping results many times a second. How fast and how reliably they can talk to each other decides how much of the expensive hardware actually gets used.

How it connects

↓ Below Power and cooling set how densely chips can be packed.

↑ Above Silicon chips are the payload. The building exists to feed them electricity, remove their heat, and wire them together.

Analogy. An orchestra: thousands of players who must stay in time. One slow player, such as a lagging chip or a flaky cable, holds up everyone.

Where it breaks: orchestra players play different parts. In the most common training setup, thousands of chips run the same computation on different slices of data, then average what they learned.

Go deeper: two networks, two speeds

Scale-up links connect GPUs inside a server or rack. NVIDIA's NVLink gives each current-generation GPU about 1.8 terabytes per second to its neighbors.

Scale-out links connect racks to each other over InfiniBand or Ethernet, at about 400–800 gigabits per second per GPU, which is roughly 50–100 gigabytes per second. That's 10–20 times slower, so engineers place the chattiest parts of a computation inside a rack and the least chatty parts across racks.

Go deeper: the fabric

Big clusters use layered "fat-tree" (Clos) networks: tiers of switches arranged so any GPU can reach any other at close to full speed. Long runs use optical transceivers that turn electrical signals into light. The biggest clusters contain hundreds of thousands of them (est.), and each one is a part that can fail.

Go deeper: training sites versus inference sites

Training wants one giant, tightly connected cluster in one place, because every chip talks to every other chip constantly. Serving answers (inference) splits into independent requests, so it can be spread across many smaller data centers closer to users.

Layer 3 of 10 · Manufacturing

Silicon

Chips are made by printing tens of billions of tiny switches onto ultra-pure silicon, using machines so complex that only one company builds the most important one.

13.5 nm
wavelength of the extreme-ultraviolet (EUV) light used to print the finest features; visible light is 400–700 nm
~$200M
for one ASML EUV scanner; the newest "High-NA" version costs roughly $350M+ (reported prices)
~90%
of the world's most advanced logic chips are made by TSMC, mostly in Taiwan (common estimate)

What it is

Quartz sand is reduced to silicon, then purified to about 99.9999999% ("nine nines"). A single crystal is pulled from the melt and sliced into 300 mm wafers. Each wafer then goes through many hundreds of steps: deposit a thin film, coat it with light-sensitive resist, print a pattern with light, etch, implant, polish, and repeat for dozens of layers. The trip takes around three months. Finally the wafer is tested and cut into individual chips (dies).

Why it matters

Smaller, better transistors mean more computation per watt and per dollar, and a large share of AI's hardware progress traces back to this layer. It is also the most concentrated part of the stack: a handful of companies in a handful of places make the parts nobody else can.

How it connects

↓ Below Fabs themselves need vast, uninterruptible power and ultra-pure water, and they're housed in some of the most controlled buildings on Earth.

↑ Above The dies cut from these wafers become GPUs, TPUs and the stacked memory chips beside them.

Analogy. Lithography is like a slide projector run in reverse: a master pattern (the mask) is shrunk four times and projected onto a wafer coated with light-sensitive chemical, like exposing photographic paper.

Where it breaks: EUV light is absorbed by glass and even by air, so there are no lenses. The light bounces off a chain of ultra-smooth mirrors inside a vacuum. And each chip needs dozens of patterned layers stacked with alignment errors of just a few nanometers.

Go deeper: how an EUV machine makes light

Tiny droplets of molten tin fall through a chamber about 50,000 times a second. A powerful CO₂ laser hits each one, turning it into a plasma hotter than the surface of the Sun, which glows at 13.5 nm. Mirrors from Zeiss collect and steer that light. Zeiss says that if one of these mirrors were scaled up to the size of Germany, its largest bump would be about 0.1 mm high.

A scanner is roughly the size of a bus and ships in dozens of containers. ASML in the Netherlands is the only supplier.

Go deeper: what "3 nm" actually means

Node names like "3 nm" and "2 nm" are marketing labels. No feature on a "3 nm" chip is 3 nm wide; transistor gates sit roughly 45–50 nm apart. The names keep counting down because density and efficiency keep improving, now mostly by changing the transistor's 3-D shape: from FinFETs to "gate-all-around" nanosheets at the 2 nm-class nodes arriving in 2025–26.

Go deeper: why the biggest chips are two chips glued together

A scanner can print an area of at most about 26 × 33 mm (roughly 850 mm²) in one exposure, so no single die can be larger. Bigger dies also catch more defects. About 60 H100-sized dies fit on a 300 mm wafer before accounting for defects.

So the newest GPUs join two large dies and several memory stacks on a shared base using advanced packaging (such as TSMC's CoWoS). Packaging capacity, not chipmaking, was one of the tightest AI bottlenecks in 2023–25.

Layer 4 of 10 · Hardware

Chips

AI runs on chips built to do enormous numbers of simple multiplications at once, and they spend much of their time waiting on memory, not math.

~1015
16-bit operations per second (a quadrillion) for an H100-class GPU from 2023; newer chips do several times more at lower precision
~300
math operations an H100 must do per byte it reads from memory to stay fully busy (≈ 1,000 TFLOPS ÷ 3.35 TB/s)

What it is

CPUs have a few dozen to about two hundred powerful cores, great at branching, step-by-step logic. GPUs have thousands of simpler cores plus tensor cores that each multiply small blocks of numbers in a single step. TPUs and other custom accelerators go further, laying out a grid of multiply-add units that pass numbers to their neighbors like a bucket brigade (a "systolic array").

Beside the processor sit stacks of high-bandwidth memory (HBM): 8 to 12 memory dies piled up and wired with thousands of connections, holding roughly 80–200+ GB per chip.

Why it matters

The vast majority of a neural network's work is matrix multiplication, and every element of a matrix product can be computed independently. Chips that do this in massive parallel set the ceiling on how big and fast AI can be. The catch is feeding them: numbers have to come from memory, and memory has not kept pace.

How it connects

↓ Below Made from silicon wafers. Each top-end chip draws about 700–1,200 W, which drives the rack and cooling design.

↑ Above Software has to arrange every computation so these units stay fed. Idle tensor cores are wasted money.

Analogy. A CPU is a few brilliant chefs who can cook anything. A GPU is thousands of line cooks, all chopping at once.

Where it breaks: the line cooks aren't independent. Groups of GPU cores must execute the same instruction at the same moment, so a GPU is only fast when the work is uniform. Matrix math happens to be perfectly uniform.

Go deeper: why matrix math, specifically

A layer of a neural network takes a list of numbers (a vector) and multiplies it by a grid of learned weights (a matrix). Process many tokens at once and the vector becomes a matrix too. Multiplying a 16,000 × 16,000 matrix by one vector is about 256 million multiply-adds, and none of them depends on another, so they can all run in parallel.

Go deeper: precision, or why fewer bits helps

Numbers can be stored with 32, 16, 8 or even 4 bits. Fewer bits mean more operations per second and less memory traffic, at some cost in accuracy. As of 2026, training mostly uses 16- and 8-bit formats, and serving increasingly uses 8- or 4-bit. Much of the headline speedup between chip generations comes from dropping precision, so compare like with like.

Go deeper: fighting the memory wall

Over 20 years, a 3× versus 1.6× trend compounds to roughly 60,000× more compute but only about 100× more bandwidth. Engineers push back with bigger on-chip caches, stacked HBM, processing many requests together (batching) so each weight fetched is reused, fusing operations so data stays on-chip, and lower precision.

Go deeper: TPUs and custom chips

Google has run its own TPUs since 2015. Amazon (Trainium), Meta (MTIA) and Microsoft (Maia) build custom accelerators too, and startups try wafer-sized chips (Cerebras) or designs that keep the model in fast on-chip memory (Groq). They trade flexibility for efficiency on a narrower set of tasks. NVIDIA GPUs still did most AI training as of 2026, with AMD as the main GPU alternative.

Layer 5 of 10 · Software

Systems software

Layers of software turn a few lines of Python into billions of precisely scheduled operations spread across thousands of chips.

~40%
of the chips' theoretical peak was actually used when training Llama 3 405B (Meta reported 38–43%); that's considered good
~3 hours
between unexpected interruptions on 16,000 GPUs (Meta: 419 in 54 days of Llama 3 training)

What it is

From the bottom: a driver lets the operating system talk to the chip. A programming platform such as NVIDIA's CUDA (since 2007) or AMD's ROCm lets programmers write kernels, small programs that run on thousands of cores at once. Libraries supply tuned kernels for matrix math and for chip-to-chip communication. Compilers (XLA, Triton, torch.compile) fuse and generate kernels automatically. Frameworks such as PyTorch and JAX let researchers write models as math and compute gradients for them automatically.

Why it matters

The same hardware can run two or three times faster or slower depending on the software. CUDA's head start, with nearly two decades of tools, libraries and trained programmers, is a big reason NVIDIA leads. And distributed-training software is what makes 16,000 separate chips behave like one computer.

How it connects

↓ Below Exposes the chips' tensor cores, memory and interconnect.

↑ Above Lets researchers describe the model and its training in a few hundred lines of code instead of hardware instructions.

Analogy. A logistics company: the framework is the order desk, the compiler plans routes, kernels are the trucks, and the communication library is the highway between warehouses.

Where it breaks: a late truck delays one package. In synchronized training, one slow GPU delays every other GPU at every step, so the whole job moves at the pace of its slowest part.

Go deeper: what a kernel is, and why fusing them helps

A kernel is one GPU program, such as "multiply these two matrices." Every time a kernel finishes, its results go back to memory, and the next kernel reads them again. Fusing several steps into one kernel keeps data on-chip. FlashAttention (2022) is the famous example: the same math as standard attention, reorganized to avoid memory round-trips, and several times faster.

Go deeper: automatic differentiation

Frameworks record every operation in the model's forward pass. To train, they replay that record backward to compute how the error depends on every weight. That's backpropagation (Layer 8), reduced to a single line of code such as loss.backward().

Go deeper: alternatives to CUDA

AMD's ROCm, Google's XLA (which drives TPUs through JAX and PyTorch), OpenAI's Triton kernel language (which targets multiple vendors), Apple's MLX and Intel's oneAPI. Switching frameworks is fairly easy; matching years of hand-tuned kernels is the hard part.

Go deeper: keeping a giant job alive

With tens of thousands of parts, something is always failing: a GPU, a memory chip, a cable, a power supply. Training jobs save checkpoints regularly and restart automatically. A nastier problem is "silent data corruption," where a faulty chip returns wrong numbers without crashing.

Layer 6 of 10 · Raw material

Data

A language model learns from trillions of words of text, which are heavily filtered and then chopped into numbered pieces called tokens.

~15 trillion
tokens used to train Meta's Llama 3 (2024). Frontier labs rarely disclose their totals.
~250,000 years
for one person to read that much, at 8 hours a day (rough estimate)
~¾ word
per token in typical English text; other languages often need more tokens

What it is

Sources include web crawls (the nonprofit Common Crawl has collected hundreds of billions of pages since 2008), code, books, scientific papers, encyclopedias, licensed data, transcripts, and synthetic text generated by other models. A pipeline extracts the text, identifies the language, removes duplicates, filters for quality, strips unsafe content and personal information, and mixes sources in chosen proportions. Finally a tokenizer turns the text into a sequence of integers.

Why it matters

A model can only reflect what's in its data. Cleaning often matters as much as quantity, and the mix shapes skills: more code makes better coders. High-quality public human text is also finite; researchers at Epoch AI have estimated it could be fully used by frontier training sometime between roughly 2026 and 2032.

How it connects

↓ Below Collected, stored and processed on the same infrastructure. Tokenizer choices get baked into the model permanently.

↑ Above Tokens become the model's inputs (Layer 7), and each next token is the answer key during training (Layer 8).

Analogy. Tokens are like syllables for a machine: common words are one piece, rare words get broken into chunks.

Where it breaks: token boundaries follow statistics of the training text, not sound or meaning. "dog", " dog" (with a space) and "Dog" can be three different tokens.

Go deeper: how filtering works

Exact and near-duplicate pages are removed (the web repeats itself enormously). Simple rules drop pages that are too short, mostly symbols, or boilerplate. Classifiers trained to recognize "educational" or well-written text score the rest. In typical published pipelines, most of the raw crawl is thrown away, often around 90% (varies widely).

Go deeper: how a tokenizer learns its vocabulary

Byte-pair encoding (BPE) starts with the 256 possible byte values, then repeatedly merges the most frequent neighboring pair into a new token, until it reaches a target vocabulary size, commonly 32,000 to 256,000 (Llama 3 uses about 128,000). Frequent words end up as single tokens; rare words stay in pieces.

Go deeper: why tokens cause odd mistakes

Ask a model how many r's are in "strawberry" and it may struggle, because it sees a couple of tokens, not ten letters. Long numbers are split into awkward chunks, which complicates arithmetic. Many non-English languages need more tokens for the same meaning, so they cost more and fit less into the model's memory.

Layer 7 of 10 · Architecture

The model

A model is a huge stack of simple arithmetic, mostly weighted sums, and its billions of weights were learned rather than written by anyone.

405 billion
parameters in Llama 3.1 405B, about 810 GB at 16 bits each. Most frontier labs don't disclose sizes.
37 of 671B
parameters used per token by DeepSeek-V3, a "mixture-of-experts" model that activates only part of itself at a time

What it is

An artificial neuron multiplies each input by a weight, adds the results, and passes the total through a simple bend (a nonlinearity). An embedding turns each token ID into a list of thousands of numbers, a position in a kind of "meaning space." The transformer (2017) then applies the same kind of block again and again, about 30 to 130 times: an attention step, where each token gathers information from earlier tokens, and a feed-forward step, where each token is processed by wide layers of neurons. At the end, the final vector is turned into a score for every token in the vocabulary.

Why it matters

The architecture decides what the model can learn efficiently. Attention lets any word draw directly on any earlier word, however far back, and it's built from matrix math that GPUs run well. That combination is why transformers took over.

How it connects

↓ Below Every block is matrix multiplication, exactly what the chips and software are built for.

↑ Above Training sets the weights (Layer 8). Inference runs them (Layer 9).

Analogy. "Neuron" is borrowed from biology, and attention is like a reader glancing back to work out what "it" refers to.

Where it breaks: a brain cell is a living cell with complex chemistry and timing; an artificial neuron is one line of arithmetic. And attention runs for every word at once, in dozens of parallel "heads" per layer, most of which do things far less tidy than resolving a pronoun.

Go deeper: attention in one paragraph

Each token produces three vectors: a query ("what am I looking for?"), a key ("what do I contain?") and a value ("what will I pass along?"). A token scores every earlier token by multiplying its query with their keys, turns the scores into weights that add up to 1 (a "softmax"), and takes the weighted average of their values. Standard attention compares every token with every other, so its cost grows with the square of the text length, one reason long contexts are expensive.

Go deeper: embeddings

Llama 3.1 405B represents each token as 16,384 numbers. Directions in this space loosely capture relationships. The famous "king − man + woman ≈ queen" comes from older word-embedding models and works only approximately, on well-chosen examples. Inside a transformer, each token's vector is rewritten layer by layer as it absorbs context.

Go deeper: where the parameters live

In a typical dense transformer, roughly two-thirds of the parameters sit in the feed-forward blocks and one-third in attention. In mixture-of-experts models, the feed-forward part is split into many "experts" and a small router sends each token to just a few, so total size and per-token cost come apart.

Go deeper: can we read the weights?

Individual weights are almost never meaningful on their own. Interpretability research finds "features" (concepts such as a specific city, or code with a bug) spread across many neurons, and is beginning to trace how some behaviors are computed. Most of what a large model does is still not understood in detail.

Layer 8 of 10 · Learning

Training

Training shows the model trillions of examples, measures how wrong each next-word guess was, and nudges every weight slightly to be less wrong.

~4 × 1025
operations to train Llama 3.1 405B (Meta: 3.8 × 1025). The largest runs as of 2026 are estimated at 1026 or more.
~31 million
H100 GPU-hours for that run; roughly 30 GWh including cooling and overhead, about what 3,000 US homes use in a year (est.)

What it is

Pretraining: take a chunk of text; at every position, the model predicts a probability for every possible next token. The loss measures how little probability it gave to the token that actually came next. Backpropagation works out how each weight contributed to that error, and gradient descent nudges every weight a tiny step in the direction that reduces it. Repeat for millions of steps, each on a batch of millions of tokens.

Post-training turns a text predictor into an assistant: fine-tuning on example conversations; learning from human preferences (RLHF: people compare answers, a reward model learns their taste, and the model is optimized against it); constitutional approaches, where the model critiques and revises its own answers against a written set of principles and AI feedback replaces some human labels; and reinforcement learning on tasks with checkable answers, such as math and code.

Why it matters

This is where capabilities come from. Scaling laws, smooth and predictable improvements in loss as compute, data and size grow, are what gave labs the confidence to spend hundreds of millions of dollars on single runs.

How it connects

↓ Below Consumes data (Layer 6) through the model's architecture (Layer 7), running on distributed software and hardware.

↑ Above Produces a fixed set of weights. Inference uses them unchanged: the model doesn't learn from your chat as it happens.

Analogy. Gradient descent is like walking downhill in thick fog, feeling the slope under your feet and stepping the steepest way down.

Where it breaks: the real landscape has billions of dimensions, not two, and it shifts slightly with every batch of data. The toy below shows the step-size problem honestly but its hills are far simpler than the real thing.

Go deeper: loss, in numbers

The loss for one prediction is −log(probability given to the right token). Give it 50% and the loss is 0.69; give it 90% and the loss is 0.11; give it 1% and the loss is 4.6. Training pushes the average down across trillions of predictions.

Go deeper: backpropagation and the "6ND" rule

Backpropagation applies the chain rule of calculus backward from the loss through every layer. The backward pass costs about twice the forward pass, so training takes about 6 operations per parameter per token: 2 forward and 4 backward. For Llama 3.1 405B: 6 × 405 billion × ~15.6 trillion tokens ≈ 3.8 × 1025.

Go deeper: scaling laws, and their limits

Loss falls as a smooth power law as compute grows (Kaplan et al., 2020). DeepMind's "Chinchilla" work (2022) found the compute-optimal recipe was about 20 training tokens per parameter. Many recent models are trained far past that (Llama 3 8B saw about 1,900 tokens per parameter) because smaller models are cheaper to run afterward.

Smooth loss does not mean smooth abilities: specific skills can seem to appear suddenly on benchmarks, though some of that is an artifact of how the benchmarks are scored. Whether scaling keeps paying off at the same rate is an open question.

Go deeper: what training costs

31 million GPU-hours at a rental price of roughly $2 an hour is about $60 million in compute (est.). The largest frontier runs are estimated to cost hundreds of millions of dollars. Those figures exclude research, failed experiments, data and staff, which can cost as much again or more.

Go deeper: the limits of post-training

Optimizing against a reward model can teach a model to please rather than to be right (sycophancy), or to find loopholes in the reward ("reward hacking"). Constitutional and AI-feedback methods scale better than pure human labeling but inherit the blind spots of the principles and the models doing the judging.

Layer 9 of 10 · Running the model

Inference

When you send a prompt, the model reads it all at once, then writes its answer one token at a time, with a full pass through the network for each token.

~0.3 Wh
for a typical short text query (2025 estimates: Google 0.24 Wh median, OpenAI ~0.34 Wh, Epoch AI ~0.3 Wh). Long reasoning can use far more.
~0.3 MB
of memory per token of context for a 70B-parameter model's "KV cache" (16-bit), so ~40 GB at 128,000 tokens

What it is

Prefill: all the prompt's tokens go through the network in parallel, and each layer's keys and values are saved in the KV cache. Decode: to produce each new token, only the newest token runs through the network, looking back at the cache instead of recomputing it. The model outputs a probability for every token in its vocabulary; a sampler picks one (the temperature setting controls how adventurous it is); the token is appended; repeat until done.

Context windows (how much text fits at once) ranged from about 128,000 to over a million tokens for leading models as of 2026.

Why it matters

Training happens once; inference happens billions of times a day. For a popular model, the total compute spent answering questions can exceed what was spent training it. Its speed and cost decide which products are possible.

How it connects

↓ Below Each new token requires reading the model's active weights from memory, so memory bandwidth (Layer 4), more than raw math, sets the speed.

↑ Above Every application is built from inference calls.

Analogy. Writing an essay one word at a time while keeping notes on everything so far. The KV cache is the notes: you never have to reread the whole thing.

Where it breaks: the "notes" aren't words; they are intermediate numbers for every layer. They grow with every token, and eventually their size limits how long a context can be and how many users one chip can serve.

Go deeper: why output tokens cost more than input tokens

Prefill handles thousands of tokens in parallel, so the chip's math units stay busy. Decode handles one token at a time per request, and each step must stream the active weights from memory. Serving many users together (batching) spreads that cost, but output is still the expensive part, so providers typically price output tokens several times higher than input.

Go deeper: the math of temperature and top-p

The model outputs a score (logit) z for each token. Probabilities are proportional to ez/T, where T is the temperature. At T = 1 you get the model's own distribution; as T approaches 0 it always picks the top token ("greedy"); above 1 the distribution flattens. Top-p sampling adds a cutoff: sample only from the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities add up to p, for example 0.9.

Go deeper: KV cache arithmetic

Bytes per token = 2 (keys and values) × layers × key-value heads × head size × bytes per number. For Llama 3 70B: 2 × 80 × 8 × 128 × 2 ≈ 330 KB. Multiply by 128,000 tokens and it's about 42 GB, for one conversation. Techniques such as grouped-query attention (already counted here), cache compression and paging keep this manageable.

Go deeper: speed, latency and "thinking"

Time to first token is typically a few hundred milliseconds to a few seconds; after that, tens to a few hundred tokens per second per user. "Reasoning" models write a long hidden chain of thought before answering. That helps on hard problems but can multiply the tokens, and so the energy, by 10 to 100 times.

Go deeper: tricks that make serving cheaper

Batching many users per chip; quantization (4- or 8-bit weights); caching the shared start of prompts; speculative decoding, where a small model drafts several tokens and the big model checks them in one pass; and distillation into smaller models. As of 2026, API prices ranged from well under $1 to tens of dollars per million output tokens, so a short answer costs a fraction of a cent to a few cents.

Layer 10 of 10 · What you touch

Applications

Applications wrap the model in instructions, memory, tools and your own data, turning next-token prediction into something useful.

~800 million
weekly ChatGPT users, as reported by OpenAI in late 2025
36%
chance a 20-step task goes right if each step is 95% reliable (0.9520), why agents need checks

What it is

Chat: hidden instructions (a system prompt) plus the conversation so far, sent to the model each turn. Coding assistants read a codebase, propose edits and run tests. Agents loop: the model decides on an action, calls a tool (search, a calculator, code, an app), reads the result, and decides again until done. Retrieval (RAG) searches your documents and pastes the relevant passages into the prompt, so answers can rest on specific sources.

Why it matters

This is where value, and risk, reaches people. Many model weaknesses (made-up facts, outdated knowledge, no access to your files) are reduced by good application design, or made worse by bad design.

How it connects

↓ Below Every step is one or more inference calls; an agentic coding task can take hundreds of calls and consume hundreds of thousands to millions of tokens (est.).

↑ Above You.

Analogy. The model is an engine; the application is the car built around it.

Where it breaks: an engine does the same thing every time. A model's output varies with sampling and phrasing and can be confidently wrong, so good applications add checks, citations, retries and human review.

Go deeper: how tool use actually works

The model is trained to emit a special structured message, such as {"tool": "search", "query": "sky color physics"}. The application, not the model, runs the search and appends the results to the conversation. The model continues from there. Open standards such as the Model Context Protocol (2024) give applications a common way to plug tools in.

Go deeper: retrieval, step by step

Documents are split into passages, and each passage is turned into an embedding vector. At question time the question is embedded too, the closest passages are found, and they're placed in the prompt with an instruction to answer from them. It reduces made-up answers but doesn't eliminate them: the search can miss, and the model can misread a source.

Go deeper: memory

Models don't remember past conversations on their own. "Memory" features are the application saving notes and pasting them back into later prompts.

Open questions and limits

What this stack still can't settle

Energy demand

Projections for AI's electricity use span a wide range because they depend on chip sales, efficiency gains and demand nobody can forecast well. Efficiency per query keeps improving, but cheaper AI tends to be used more. Local grids feel the strain first, and the carbon impact depends on whether new supply is gas, nuclear or renewables.

Supply-chain concentration

One company makes EUV scanners, one foundry makes most leading-edge chips, and three companies make nearly all high-bandwidth memory. A disruption to any of them, whether a disaster, a conflict or an export rule, would ripple through the whole stack.

What models still get wrong

They can state false things fluently ("hallucination"), stumble on tasks that look trivial (counting letters, some arithmetic) because of tokens, know nothing after their training cutoff unless given tools, tell people what they want to hear, and follow instructions hidden in documents they read ("prompt injection"). Errors compound over long agent tasks.

What we don't understand

We can train models far more easily than we can explain them. Whether scaling keeps delivering, how to measure real ability rather than benchmark memorization, what happens when high-quality human text runs short, and how copyright and consent apply to training data are all unresolved.
